# Abstract  

Experience replay, characterized by the sequential reactivation of hippocampal place cells, has been proposed to consolidate past experiences and simulate future scenarios, thereby constructing cognitive maps to guide action. However, the role of experience replay in the formation of hexagonal patterns of entorhinal grid cells – known to serve as the metrics of cognitive map – remains largely unknown. Here, we used continuous attractor models to simulate the formation of multi-scale hexagonal patterns, and investigated the regularity of generated patterns by directly modulating experience replay of hippocampal place cells in awake state. We found that reverse replay significantly increased the regularity of small-scale hexagonal patterns compared to conditions with no replay, forward replay, and shuffled replay of past experiences. In contrast, large-scale hexagonal patterns emerged spontaneously, independent of experience replay. Further analysis revealed that the efficiency of reverse replay in hexagonal pattern formation was influenced by the interaction between grid scales and the amount of past experience. Specifically, reverse replay maintained excitatory and inhibitory grid cell activity during pattern translation when the amount of past experience was limited, making it particularly efficient for the rapid formation of small-scale hexagonal patterns. In summary, our results suggest a potential link between experience replay and the formation of multi-scale hexagonal patterns from a computational perspective. These findings may provide insights into the role of grid cell signals in visual attention and the rapid adaptation of hexagonal patterns in scene transition.  

# Introduction  

Episodic memory, closely associated with the hippocampus (HPC; Tulving and Markowitsch, 1998; Burgess et al., 2002), is metaphorically described as mental travel on a cognitive map (Tolman, 1948; Moser et al., 2017). This cognitive ability depends on the place cell replay in the hippocampus, which organizes experiences into relational structures for retrieving the past and imagining the future (Fig.1a; Foster and Wilson, 2006; Diba and Buzsáki, 2007; Schacter et al., 2007; Carr et al., 2011; Mattar and Daw, 2018; Pfeiffer, 2020). The sequential representations of the hippocampal replay have been associated with the topological structure of novel environment (Wu and Foster, 2014), the factorized representation of objects (Liu et al., 2019), the reorganization of non-local experiences into planning (Liu et al., 2021), and compositional computation (Kurth-Nelson et al., 2023). Impaired hippocampal replay leads to deficits in sequential representations, as observed in both schizophrenia patients (Nour et al., 2021) and rodent models (Suh et al., 2013). However, the mechanism of hippocampal replay in constructing a cognitive map has been less discussed.  

The relational structures of experience, fundamental to the cognitive map (Hafting et al., 2005; McNaughton et al., 2006; Behrens et al., 2018), are associated with the entorhinal grid cells to map the external environment with hexagonal metrics (Fig.1c; Bellmund et al., 2019; Bellmund et al., 2020; Peer et al., 2021; Whittington et al., 2022), where experiences are represented by the multi-hexagonal scales underlying positional encoding (Fiete et al., 2008; Bush et al., 2015; Stemmler et al., 2015; Bicanski and Burgess, 2019). These hexagonal structures have shown strong associations with place cell activity. In the spatial domain, the hexagonal regularity and spatial scale of grid cells decreased as a function of environmental novelty (Barry et al., 2012), which has been known to track the firing rate of CA1 place cells (Larkin et al., 2014). In the time domain, grid cell activity was elicited by visual attention within a time range of milliseconds to seconds (Nau et al., 2018; Killian & Buffalo, 2018; Staudigl et al., 2018; Julian et al., 2018; Wilming et al., 2018), implicating the capacity of grid cell in rapid forming the hexagonal patterns. Meanwhile, the hippocampal replays of place cell were observed within the same time range, either during movements on a linear-track (Pfeiffer, 2020) or following the first lap of a U-shaped track (Foster & Wilson 2006) in goal-directed navigation. Particularly, early postnatal development suggests that grid cells emerge abruptly in the awake state around day P20, followed by the reliable emergence of sequential replay between P19 and P20 (Farooq & Dragoi, 2019). In mature brains,  

grid cells and place cells were coordinated during hippocampal replay. Grid cell activity was spatially coherent with place cell activity, with an average of 11ms delay, suggesting a propagation of replay sequence from the hippocampus to the entorhinal cortex in awake state (Ólafsdóttir et al., 2016). These empirical findings may reflect a major involvement of hippocampal replay in forming the hexagonal regularity and the multi-scale organization of grid cell.  

To test this conjecture, we simulated the formation of multi-scale hexagonal pattern of grid cell using continuous attractor networks (CAN) (Burak and Fiete, 2009; Couey et al., 2013; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019; Khona et al., 2021), which is commonly referred to as the classic spin-glass model (Fuhs and Touretzky, 2006). Meanwhile, hippocampal replay was simulated by sequential place cell activity that represents past visited spatial locations. A place cell at each location was modelled to connect with a grid cell from one of four spatial scales (Fig.1c, scale [i.e., Mod] I, II, III, and IV; Fiete et al., 2008; Stemmler et al., 2015; Bush et al., 2015). The excitatory and inhibitory synaptic plasticity of grid cells was independently updated within each spatial scale. With the endeavor proving challenging in empirical studies, we examined the regularity of the generated hexagonal pattern, indexed by grid score, under four types of replay conditions (i.e., No-, Forward-, Reverse-, and shuffled-replay) and two types of experiences (i.e., limited or extended exploration defined either by the number of locations or directions). Limited exploration was intentionally used to investigate the rapid formation of hexagonal patterns in eye movements (Nau et al., 2018; Killian & Buffalo, 2018; Staudigl et al., 2018; Julian et al., 2018; Wilming et al., 2018) and one-dimensional linear tracks (Yoon et al., 2016; Jacob et al., 2019) as compared to full exploration of the environment (i.e., extended exploration; Hafting et al., 2005; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019). The present design allows investigations of the potential link between experience replay and the formation of hexagonal patterns of grid cell by intentionally activating or deactivating place cell activity.  

# Results  

We first examined the effect of No-replay, Forward-replay, and Reverse-replay on the formation of hexagonal patterns under limited exploration. The model received a sequence of place cell activations as inputs, simulating "walking" experiences during pattern translation in randomized allocentric directions (i.e., 70°, 115°, or 160° with 0° pointing East) (Fig.2a). Each "walking" experience consisted of either 5, 10, or 15 location movements, resulting in an average of 10 location movements in each direction and a total of 1200 movements in each simulation. The final visit of each experience was designated as the pause locations to mimic the animal's decision point. In the No-replay condition, only the actual "walking" experiences were applied to the model. In contrast, the Forward-replay and Reverse-replay conditions examined the direction-dependency of replay. In these two conditions, the model received an additional "walking" experience identical to the actual one, preceding the movements in the forward direction and following the movements in the reverse direction, respectively (Foster & Wilson, 2006; Ambrose et al., 2016; Gillespie et al., 2021). The spatial regularity of the hexagonal patterns generated in each condition was assessed through 15 independent simulations.  

The result revealed that the Reverse-replay condition promoted the formation of hexagonal patterns across all grid scales. The grid scores showed a rapid increase at the beginning of simulations and remained significant over 1200 movements (Fig.2b & c & Fig.S2a, mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II / Mod III / IV = 1.39 / 1.44 / 1.43 / 1.39, respectively; p<0.05, initial threshold, p < 0.05; corrected for multiple comparisons). Next, we further accessed the group-level regularity of hexagonal patterns using the toroidal topology detection procedure (Gardner et al., 2022). The joint activity of rats' grid cells was found to reside on a toroidal manifold, consistent with prediction of the twisted torus model (Samsonovich and McNaughton, 1997; McNaughton et al., 2006; Knierim and Zhang, 2012; Khona & Fiete, 2022; Gardner et al., 2022; Sorscher et al., 2023). Specifically, we observed a significant toroidal structure based on the dimensionality-reduced population activity of CAN-generated grid cells in each spatial scale (Fig.2d, Fig.S3, see Method for details). In contrast, the No-replay and Forward-replay conditions resulted in deficits in hexagonal pattern formation particularly at small scales (i.e., grid Mod I & II; No-replay: Fig.2e & Fig.S2a, left panel, mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II = 0.49 / 0.77; Forward-replay: Fig.2f & Fig.S2a, left panel, mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II = 0.29 / 0.64). For both conditions, the grid scores of small-scale hexagonal patterns fluctuated during pattern translation after a transient increase at the beginning of simulations and eventually decreased, yielding a stripe pattern (Yang et al., 2004). By comparison, these two conditions generated regular hexagonal patterns at large scales (Mod III & IV), where grid scores were significantly higher than the shuffled threshold (No-replay: Fig.2e & Fig.S2a, right panel; mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II = 1.42 / 1.29; Forward-replay: Fig.2f & Fig.S2a, right panel, mean grid score of Mod III & IV = 1.32 / 1.30; p<0.05, initial threshold, p < 0.05; corrected for multiple comparisons).  

To further investigate the impact of spatial-temporal structure of past experiences, we tested a Shuffled-experience condition in response to reverse replay. In this condition, replay directions were randomly drawn from past experiences within the simulation and were not necessarily reversed relative to the immediate past "walking" experience. The Shuffled-replay condition produced results similar to the No-replay and Forward-replay conditions. Stripe patterns were observed at small scales (i.e., grid Mod I & Mod II; Fig.2g & Fig.S2a, left panel, mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II = 0.29 / 0.53), whereas significant regularity of hexagonal patterns was observed at large scales (i.e., grid Mod III & Mode IV; Fig.2g & Fig.S2a, right panel, mean grid score of Mod III / Mod IV = 1.40 / 1.36; p < 0.05, initial threshold, p < 0.05; corrected for multiple comparisons).  

Taken together, the No-replay, Forward-replay, and Shuffled-replay conditions produced significantly higher grid scores for large scale hexagonal patterns compared to that of small scales (Mod I & II versus Mod III & IV: t (14) = 7.02 / 13.89 / 13.28 for No-replay, Forward-replay, and Shuffled-replay, respectively, p < 0.001, two-sided), and the hexagonal patterns emerged from stripe patterns at small scales in the Reverse-replay condition. These findings suggest that the formation of multi-scale hexagonal patterns selectively favors Reverse-replay, characterized by a sequential replay opposite to actual experience. This presumably reflect the necessity of reverse experience replay in constructing a high-resolution cognitive map.

Next, we examined the impact of reverse replay on the regularity of hexagonal patterns by linearly modulating the amount of experience (AE) and grid scales (GS). Specifically, we explored how grid scores varied as a function of the AE, indexed by the number of visited locations in each direction (Fig.3a, Left), and the GS, indexed by the size of grid cell receptive fields (Fig.3a, Right), under both No-replay and Reverse-replay condition. To achieve this, we conducted 1,160 CAN simulations on grid scores, systematically varying the modulator AE, GS, and the type of experience replay across 29 levels (6-90 locations [i.e., pixels]), 20 levels (11-30 pixels), and 2 conditions (No- & Reverse-replay), respectively (see Methods for details).  

As expected, the results revealed significant main effects of replay (F(1, 1152) = 147.5, P < 0.001) and GS (F(1, 1152) = 1376.2, P < 0.001), both positively correlated with grid scores (Fig.S6), consistent with findings observed during limited exploration. Interestingly, a significant main effect of AE was also identified (Fig.S6; F(1, 1152) = 324.8, P < 0.001), with grid scores negatively correlated with AE in the Reverse-replay condition (t(576) = -16.503, P < 0.001), and maintaining constant in the No-replay condition (t(576) = -1.910, P = 0.057). These results highlighted the conditional efficiency of reverse replay in hexagonal pattern formation, dependent on AE, as indicated by a significant interaction (F(1, 1152) = 155.9, P < 0.001). To further assess AE's influence in the Reverse-replay condition, we calculated the decay rate of grid scores along AE, indexed by the slope $k$, and identified the smallest AE for each grid scale at which grid scores reached the significance threshold from the initial state (Fig.S7). The analysis revealed a significantly positive correlation between the decay slope $k$ and grid scales (R²= 0.62, P < 0.001), indicating that smaller scale hexagonal patterns were associated with faster grid score decay (Fig.3b). Additionally, the decay slope $k$ accurately predicted the AE preceding the experiences replay required to form regular hexagonal patterns (Fig.3c; R2=98.66%, P<0.001), demonstrating that large scale hexagonal pattern formed with small AEs, while small scale patterns required larger AEs. The maximum AE that enables the formation of small scale hexagonal pattern was approximately 25 locations, corresponding to an AE size ranging between half the space's edge and half its diagonal. This reflected the edge effect in hexagonal pattern formation (McNaughton et al., 2006; Foster and Wilson, 2006; Sorscher et al., 2019; Hägglund et al., 2019; Sorscher et al., 2023). In contrast, large scale hexagonal patterns formed rapidly with smaller AEs and were unaffected by edge effects (Fig.3c).  

The above findings confirm the positive impact of reverse replay on the regularity of small scale hexagonal patterns across varying amount of past experiences (i.e., number of locations in a given direction). Furthermore, these results suggest a potential biological mechanism for the rapid tessellation of high resolution cognitive maps, resembling memory consolidation (Carr et al., 2011). Specifically, the reversed sequential activity of place cells appears to mitigate edge effect by activating connected grid cells, maintaining the excitatory and inhibitory dynamics of new-formed receptive fields. Through this mechanism, an infinitely large space can be folded into a periodic structure and represented by a limited number of neurons. For example, in the absence of reverse replay (Fig.3d, top row), the newly-formed receptive field at T5 lost its excitatory and inhibitory dynamics during pattern translation at T7, as exceeding the boundary of representational space. This disruption led to the disintegration of the developing hexagonal pattern into a stripe-like pattern (Fig.3d, top row; T9-T13). In contrast, reverse replay of past experiences preserved the receptive field by transferring location representations to neurons encoding previously visited locations (Fig.3d, bottom row; T9-T13). This process facilitated the continued development of the hexagonal pattern. On the other hand, large scale receptive fields were rapidly formed between T3 and T5 (Fig.3d, top row). At T7, a newly formed receptive field replaced the earlier ones that had propagated out of the representational space during pattern translation. This substitution prevented hexagonal pattern disruption even in the absence of reverse replay (Fig.3e, top row). In summary, our simulations demonstrate that reverse replay plays a critical role in balancing spatial (i.e., grid scale) and temporal (amount of past experience) demands for hexagonal pattern generation.

To further validate the mechanism of reverse replay in representational transferring among neurons during pattern formation, we simulated the formation of hexagonal patterns under conditions of extended movement directions and the absence of reverse replay. The rationale for this analysis was based on the hypothesis implicated by the above results that high-frequency and diverse directional switching is more likely to maintain newly formed receptive fields within the representational space, thereby avoiding edge effect, compared to scenarios with homogeneous movement directions. If this hypothesis holds true, regular small scale hexagonal patterns should be generated despite the absence of reverse replay. Therefore, this analysis was conducted using an open-source dataset contributed by Hafting et al (2005) (Fig.4a), which contains a large number of animal's trajectories with diverse movement directions uniformly distributed across 0° to 360° (Fig.4b; p = 0.23, Rayleigh test). The trajectories were down-sampled to 1200 movement locations and scaled into the 45-by-45-pixel representational space to match the procedure used in earlier simulations. As expected, we observed a significant regularity in hexagonal patterns across all grid scales, even in the absence of reverse replay (Fig.4c, grid score= 1.46/1.47/1.38/1.29 for Mod I / II / III / IV, respectively. p < 0.05, initial threshold, p < 0.05; corrected for multiple comparisons). Notably, this result does not imply the absence of reverse replay when rats collect scattered randomly thrown pieces of food (Hafting et al., 2005). Instead, it highlights the potential neural mechanism underlying hexagonal pattern formation, suggesting that grid cells use representational transfer to maintain the excitatory and inhibitory dynamics of receptive fields.  

Saturated exploration is not required in many scenarios. Therefore, we conducted a final simulations of hexagonal pattern formation by converging the range of movement directions into homogeneity. We predicted that stripe-like patterns would emerge in the absence of reverse replay at small scales, while the presence of reverse replay would restore the regularity of hexagonal patterns. To test this, we defined eight global directions that cover 360° with 45° intervals (Fig.4d, e). Each global direction condition was repeated twice (Fig.S8), and were paired with a series of intermediate "walking" experiences that included random noises between -90° and +90° (Fig.4f). This approach maintained the diversity in movement directions while excluding the possibility of representational transfer. As expected, the result revealed stripe-like patterns for small scales in the No-replay condition (Fig.4g, mean grid score of Mod I / Mod II = 0.49 / 0.41, respectively), leading to a significantly lower grid score than those of large scales (Fig.S2b; t(15) = 7.47, p<0.001, two-sided). In contrast, multi-scale hexagonal patterns emerged in the Reverse-replay condition  

(Fig.4h & Fig.S2b, mean grid score= 1.32 / 1.28 / 1.39 / 1.32, for Mod I / II / III / IV, respectively. p < 0.05, initial threshold, p < 0.05; corrected for multiple comparisons), showing significantly higher overall grid score than those in the No-replay condition across grid scales (Fig.4h, t (15) = 8.85, p<0.001, two-sided). In summary, these results demonstrate a potential critical role of reverse replay in maintaining the newly formed receptive fields of hexagonal patterns during pattern translation, particularly for high resolution representation.  

# Discussion  

The present study utilized CAN models to investigate the role of experience replay in facilitating the hexagonal pattern formation of grid cells. We found that reverse experience replay, as opposed to no replay, forward replay, or shuffled-experience replay, promoted the formation of multi-scale hexagonal patterns. This effect was particularly effective in enhancing the spatial regularity of small-scale hexagonal patterns. Thus, these findings presumably reflect the role of place cell replay in constructing high-resolution metrics of grid cells (Schuck and Niv, 2019). Further analyses revealed that the efficiency of reverse replay in hexagonal pattern formation was influenced by the interplay of grid scales and the amount of experience (i.e., limited versus extended environmental exploration). This finding demonstrated that regular hexagonal pattern formation depends on balancing the spatial and temporal demands of pattern generation. Specifically, reverse replay prevents the disruption of generated receptive fields caused by the edge effects during wave propagation by using representational transfer, which folds space into a grid in a periodic manner. This computational mechanism might be the key to achieving infinite space tessellation with a finite number of neurons.  

The formation of abstract knowledge has been strongly associated with both hippocampal experience replay (Wu and Foster, 2014; Liu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Kurth-Nelson et al., 2023) and entorhinal grid cells (McNaughton et al., 2006; Behrens et al., 2018; Bellmund et al., 2019; Bellmund et al., 2020; Peer et al., 2021; Whittington et al., 2022). The present study provides computational evidence supporting their potential link and offers two key implications. First, the mechanism underlying the formation of small to large hexagonal patterns may not follow a linear progression but rather a categorical distinction. Consistent with the reaction-diffusion model, where Turing-instability varies non-linearly as a function of spatial frequency (Yang et al., 2004), our results showed that large scale hexagonal patterns spontaneously emerged independent of experience replay, whereas small scale hexagonal patterns depended on experience replay. Anatomically, the spatial scales of both entorhinal grid cell and hippocampal place cells decrease along the anteroposterior (ventral-dorsal) axis, with the posterior area, a particularly common recording site for experience replay (Carr et al., 2012; Pfeiffer & Foster, 2013; Ambrose et al., 2016; Gillespie et al., 2021), representing fine-grained experiences compared to the anterior area, which encodes coarse experiences (Hafting et al., 2005; Stensola et al., 2012; Strange et al., 2014; Schiller et al., 2015). For instance, hippocampal sharp wave ripples, which facilitate experience  

replay, are typically characterized by transient high-frequency oscillations for precise location coding (Buzsáki, 2015). These findings align well with our observations that small-scale hexagonal pattern formation relies on experience replay for fine-grained spatial representation.  

Another implication of the present work is that the rapid formation of high-resolution hexagonal metrics is likely associated with hippocampal experience replay. CAN models have been extensively used to simulate hexagonal patterns (Hafting et al., 2005; Burak and Fiete, 2009; Couey et al., 2013; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019; Khona et al., 2021; Sorscher et al., 2023). However, simulations of regular hexagonal patterns have typically been based on saturated environmental exploration, assuming that animal's foraging behavior involves visiting a large number of spatial locations and directions. Interestingly, grid cell activity has been observed in one-dimensional linear track (Yoon et al., 2016) and circular track (Jacob et al., 2019), despite the constraints that these experimental environments impose on direction or location. Moreover, grid cell activity has been elicited by eye movements during visual attention tasks (Nau et al., 2018; Killian & Buffalo, 2018; Staudigl et al., 2018; Julian et al., 2018; Wilming et al., 2018), demonstrating the capacity of grid cell to map visual space. For instance, in Staudigl (2018), grid cells mapped eye movements during freely viewing 100 indoor and outdoor images. Intuitively, high-resolution hexagonal metrics are required to meet the task demands of identifying small objects within rich image textures. These findings demonstrate that the hexagonal patterns of grid cells can be rapidly formed under spatial and temporal constraints during environmental exploration.  

In addition to the implications of the present study, our results can not fully account for the biological realism and plausibility of the hippocampal-entorhinal circuit. One limitation is that we selectively simulated the unidirectional impacts of place cell replay on the formation of hexagonal patterns. Consequently, our explanations were based on the predefined location representations of place cells. However, grid cells and place cells are known to function as a complementary and interacting system that supports the reliable coding of external space (Solstad et al., 2006; Brun et al., 2008; Bush et al., 2014; Bush et al., 2015; Bicanski & Burgess, 2019). Therefore, future studies should explore the bidirectional interactions between the hexagonal patterns of grid cells and place cell replay. Another aspect concerns the maturation of grid cells and place cell replay observed in biological system. While there is some overlap in their maturation around postnatal day 20 (Wills & Cacucci, 2014; Farooq & Dragoi, 2019), previous studies have indicated that grid cell activity  

can be observed in rats as young as P16 (Langston et al., 2010; Wills et al., 2010), despite the irregularity of hexagonal patterns at this stage. One possible explanation is that these early grid-like cells represent coarse spatial representations that are independent of hippocampal experience replay. Future research should address these unsolved issues by modelling a unified hippocampal-entorhinal circuit to investigate the simultaneous development of place cells and grid cells.  

# Materials and Methods  

# Simulation of multi-scale hexagonal pattern  

Multi-scale hexagonal patterns of grid cell were simulated on an n-by-n-pixel square representational space ξ using CAN model (Fuhs and Touretzky, 2006; Burak and Fiete, 2009; Couey et al., 2013; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019; Torch Version 1.13, Python version 3.9), where n=45 pixels. Each pixel i in the space, representing a spatial location (x, y), corresponded to a place cell i, which was connected to a downstream grid cell i at each spatial scale (Bonnevie et al., 2013; Rennó-Costa and Tort, 2017). Specifically, a series of "walking" experience were included in a single CAN simulation. Each experience, defined by numeric spatial movements with a movement step size λ of 3 pixels in a given spatial direction θ, was designed to activate a sequence of place cells. These place cell activities generated a displacement vector signal v at each location during CAN pattern translation, indicating which grid cell would be activated when approaching adjacent locations (Equation 1).  

$$

v = \lambda \cdot ( \cos ( \theta ) , \sin ( \theta ) )

$$  

The procedure for synaptic plasticity updates during CAN pattern translation is illustrated in Equation 2. The membrane voltage $\xi$ of grid cell $i$, simulated as a two-dimensional n-by-n matrix, was initialized at zero. Upon receiving place cell activity, $\xi_i$ was scaled to simulate the firing rate $f_i$ of the neuron if the voltage strength exceeded the threshold $\eta$ (i.e., $\eta = 0$; Equation 3; see also table S1). Otherwise, $\xi_i$ was inhibited to zero using a nonlinear transfer function at that location. Thus, all active location representations of grid cell $i$ that surpassed the threshold $\eta$ across the space were identified. These firing rates were weighted by the predefined excitatory and inhibitory matrix $W_{ij}$, which represents the periodic connection strengths of grid cell $i$ with grid cells $j$ that encoded these locations (Fig.S4). This process formed the conjunctive activity update for $\xi$ by summing the weighted firing rates of grid cells $j$. The procedure was repeated over spatial movements until the number of movements within a simulation reached the predefined limit (1200 movements). The time constant $\tau$ was set to 10 ms. The noise $\varepsilon$ in the pattern displacement vector $\nu$ was applied in the "extended exploration" condition, while $\varepsilon$ was set to zero in the "limited exploration" condition.  

$$

\tau \frac { d \xi _ { i } } { d t } = - \xi _ { i } + \sum _ { j } W _ { i j } f _ { j } + v _ { i } + \varepsilon

$$  

$$

f _ { i } = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { \sqrt { \xi _ { i } } , } & { \xi _ { i } > \eta } \\ { 0 , } & { \xi _ { i } \leq \eta } \end{array} \right.

$$  

The recurrent weight $W$, represented as a two-dimensional symmetric matrix, was simulated using the representational oscillatory interference of elementary neurons proposed by the oscillatory interference model (Fig.S4 & Table.S1; Burgess et al., 2007; Burgess, 2008; Bush & Burgess, 2014). This model hypothesizes the involvement of velocity-controlled oscillator neurons (VCO), whose burst-firing strength varies with spatial directions and movement speed, such that displacement in a given direction produces band-like or stripe-like firing patterns. Specifically, the burst-firing maps of VCOs in direction $\theta$ are formalized as $\cos\left(\omega\left(v_{x}X + v_{y}Y\right)\right)$ (Equation 4), where $v_{x}$ and $v_{y}$, derived from $\cos\left(\theta\right)$ and $\sin\left(\theta\right)$, determines the preferred displacement direction of the activity gradient $v_{x}X + v_{y}Y$. Here, X and Y denote two-dimensional matrices in which each element represents the x- and y-coordinate, respectively. At each location $(x, y)$, a recurrent weight $W_{(x, y)}$ was generated by linearly summing the burst-firing maps of VCOs at spatial frequency $\omega$, representing all possible directions radiating from $(x, y)$. The spatial frequency $\omega$, defined in Equation 5, depends on grid scales $\sigma$, which represents Euclidean distance between two adjacent periodic activity peaks and is positively related to receptive field size. Grid scale of 11, 15, 22, 30 pixels, equivalent to 24%, 33%, 49%, and 67% of the representational space size, respectively, were selected for simulating the multi-scale hexagonal patterns based on a grid ratio of $\sqrt{2}$, as suggested by previous studies (Stemmler et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2015; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019). For instance, the grid scale $\sigma = 30$ (i.e., 30 pixels) corresponds to a relatively lower spatial frequency compared to $\sigma = 11$. These grid scales were treated as independent during CAN simulation.  

$$

W _ { ( x , y ) } = \sum _ { \theta = 0 } ^ { \infty } \alpha \cos \left( \omega ( \nu _ { x } X + \nu _ { y } Y ) \right)

$$  

$$

\omega = 2 p i / \sigma

$$  

The periodic repeating pattern of grid cells are known to reside on torus topology (Samsonovich and McNaughton, 1997; McNaughton et al., 2006; Knierim and Zhang, 2012; Khona & Fiete, 2022; Gardner et al., 2022; Sorscher et al., 2023). This topology is conceptualized by connecting the opposite edges of a square representational space, implying that the periodic activity of grid cells transcends boundary effects when moving along a straight line in the space. To simulate this periodic boundary condition, we assumed that the periodic boundaries are a neural characteristic of adult-like grid cells. These neurons should exhibit significant grid scores relative to the shuffled threshold, ensuring that the regular periodicity of hexagonal patterns maintains accurate location displacements at opposite edge when the CAN model approaches to boundary. For this simulation, we extracted the grid orientation, estimated using the autocorrelation maps of grid cells at each movement step (Fig.S5). With grid orientation and grid scale determined, we interpolated grid activity at each edge location based on adjacent grid activity along the three main axes of the hexagonal pattern. This method predicted when and where specific phases of the hexagonal pattern would appear at the edges, based on the known grid scale and orientation.  

# Simulation of hippocampal replay  

To examine the impact of experience replay on the regularity of multi-scale hexagonal patterns generated by CAN, we intentionally manipulated the sequential activity of place cells at the "decision point" that mimics animal's decision point (denoted by the red dots in Fig.2a, e-g). The place cell activity represents the experience of sequential visited locations with either reverse, forward, or shuffled spatial directions relative to each of actual past experience. The No-replay condition was used as control, where no additional activity input of place cell was applied to CAN simulation. In the Forward-/Reverse-replay condition, CAN received the sequential activity of place cells indicating the identical/reverse directions prior to/next to the actual movements, respectively. The Shuffled-replay condition examines the necessity of the spatial and temporal structure of past experience during experience replay, where the sequential activity of place cells from past experiences were shuffled in temporal domain before sending to CAN, and were reversely replayed at the "decision point" during pattern translation. Each simulation of experience replay consisted of a series of "walking" experiences, each of which was randomly paired with either 5-, 10-, or 15-movement locations along the spatial direction determined by the sequential activity of place cells. The total number of movements in each simulation was constrained to 1200  

movements. The movement trajectories of CAN were finally projected on a squared space created by Unity (Unity Technologies, San Francisco) for visualization purpose.  

In addition to the type of replay, we examined the impact of amount of experience of place cell replay on multi-scale hexagonal pattern formation during limited and extended exploration. The limited exploration experiment aims to examine the possibility of the rapid formation of grid-like representational space observed during eye movements (Nau et al., 2018; Killian & Buffalo, 2018; Staudigl et al., 2018; Julian et al., 2018; Wilming et al., 2018) and the movements in one-dimensional linear and circular track (Yoon et al., 2016; Jacob et al., 2019). We constrained the movement experience to three spatial directions, each separated by 45° (70°, 115°, and 160° with 0° pointing towards East). Each "walking" experience was randomly assigned with one out of the three spatial direction. Meanwhile, the length of movements within each experience remained unchanged (i.e., either 5-, 10-, or 15-locations). This design excluded the possibility of the confounding effect that the "walking" direction is opposite to actual experience, which is identical to the impact of reverse replay, although the walking experiences with reversed movement direction have been commonly involved during saturated environmental exploration (Hafting et al., 2005; Burak and Fiete, 2009; Couey et al., 2013; Kang and Balasubramanian, 2019; Khona et al., 2021; Sorscher et al., 2023). To ensure the robustness of limited exploration simulation, each type of experience replay was independently simulated 15 times before group-level analysis.  

Three "extended exploration" experiments were conducted to examine the impact of reverse replay on multi-scale hexagonal pattern formation compared to that of no replay under a large number of walking directions or locations. Firstly, we examined the impact of AE and grid scale on the regularity of hexagonal patterns (i.e., how grid score changes as a function of AEs and grid scales). A total of 29 AEs, ranged from 2 to 30 locations with a step size of 1, and a total of 20 grid scales, ranged from 11 to 30 pixels (Table 1), were prepared. This resulted in a total of 1160 simulations with 15 simulations for each AE-grid scale pair. The impact of AEs and grid scales on hexagonal pattern formation was assessed by the slope $-\frac{1}{k}$ of exponential decay function $ae^{(-\frac{AE}{k})}$, which was calculated in each grid scale and type of replay, and predicts how fast grid score reaches the significance threshold within the simulation given AE, grid scale and type of replay. The slopes were first linearized via log transformation and was subsequently fitted by the curve_fit function of scipy package (version 1.9.3). Secondly, an open-source dataset (Fig.4a; Hafting et al., 2005; https://www.ntnu.edu/kavli/research/grid-cell-data) was used to examine the  

multi-scale hexagonal regularity during extended exploration in the No-replay condition. The datasets included a large number of movement trajectories of rats as they collected randomly scattered pieces of food in an open square-shaped environment, and thus offering diversely-distributed movement directions ranged from 0° - 360°. The total number of locations of rat movements were down-sampled to 1200. Finally, we examined the impact of reverse replay on hexagonal pattern formation under a large number of walking locations and a constrained number of walking directions. In this experiment, the "walking" experiences were constrained by one out of eight predefined global directions, each 45° apart (Fig.4d; 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°, 225°, 270°, and 315°), plus an random noise ϵ between -90° to 90°. This resulted in a large number of walking locations with walking directions constrained in 180° (Fig.4f), thus excluding the possibility of the replay direction opposite to actual experience. To ensure the robustness of simulations, 16 simulations were independently performed (Fig.4d; each global direction was repeated twice) before group-level analysis.  

# Validation of grid regularity  

The spatial regularity of hexagonal patterns, as indexed by grid score, was computed with the following procedures introduced previously (Sargolini et al., 2006; Barry & Burgess, 2017). The spatial autocorrelation of each hexagonal pattern was estimated by Equation 6, which calculated Pearson's product moment correlation coefficient for each representational location (x, y) of hexagonal pattern based on the neural activity $\lambda(x, y)$ and the spatial lags $\tau_x$ and $\tau_y$ over n locations. The autocorrelogram was further rotated by 30°, 60°, 90°, 120°, and 150°, the similarity between the rotated autocorrelogram and the original autocorrelogram was assessed by Pearson correlation. Grid score was finally derived by subtracting the highest similarity of 30°, 90° and 150° from the minimum similarity of 60° and 120°.  

$$

r ( \tau _ { x } , \tau _ { y } ) = \frac { n \sum \lambda ( x , y ) \, \lambda ( x - \tau _ { x } , y - \tau _ { y } ) - \sum \lambda ( x , y ) \, \lambda ( x - \tau _ { x } , y - \tau _ { y } ) } { \sqrt { n \sum \lambda ( x , y ) ^ { 2 } ( \sum \lambda ( x , y ) ) ^ { 2 } } \sqrt { n \sum \lambda ( x - \tau _ { x } , y - \tau _ { y } ) ^ { 2 } ( \sum \lambda ( x - \tau _ { x } , y - \tau _ { y } ) ) ^ { 2 } } }

$$  

To assess the hexagonal pattern regularity of grid cell population, we followed the analytical pipeline of persistent cohomology (Ghrist, 2008; Gardner et al., 2022), using the package Ripser++ (Version 1.1.2) and umap-learn (Version 0.5.7) (Zhang et al., 2020; Bauer, 2021). For each grid scale, persistent cohomology was used to identify the potential toroidal  

structure in the population activity of grid cells, which has been shown to exist in the grid cell population of biological brain (Gardner et al., 2022). Specifically, the high-dimension representation of CAN-generated grid cells were first reduced to six dimensions using principal component analysis (PCA). This linear embedding preserved the hexagonal structures and mitigated the "curse of dimensionality". Subsequently, a second nonlinear dimensionality reduction step was applied, reducing the data to three dimensions for point-cloud visualization of grid cell activity. This step utilized UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction) with the following hyperparameters: 'n_components'=3, metric='cosine', 'min_dist'=0.8, 'init'='spectral' (Gardner et al., 2022). In the persistence analysis, cohomology was used to detect the global topological structure of the Z-transformed three-dimensional embedding, characterized by a toroidal structure in Euclidean space. In doing so, each point in the point cloud were replaced with a ball of infinitesimal radius. Upon increasing the radius of balls, the balls are gradually expanded to form certain global structure with holes of different dimensions. The lifespans of the balls, indicated by the range of radii from when they appeared to when they disappeared, were represented by the lengths of the bars. (i.e., barcodes). A toroidal structure is expected to have one 0D hole (the connected object[i.e., entire torus]), two 1D holes (the inner and outer hole of torus), and one 2D hole (the cavity in the interior of torus). Therefore, the hexagonal patterns of CAN-generated grid cells were considered regular if the expected number of holes are detected and persisted for a sufficiently long duration during ball radius expansion, compared to the significance threshold derived from permutation. To minimize the noise of dimensionality reduction caused by the imbalanced representation of spatial locations given the arbitrary size of grid cell population in the 45-by-45-pixel representational space and grid scales, not all CAN-generated grid cells were used in the persistent cohomology analysis. Instead, for each spatial scale, we selected a population of grid cell to ensure that each location was represented by an approximately equivalent number of receptive fields (Fiete et al., 2008), considering grid orientation and grid scales. As a result, groups of neurons, 117 neurons, 247 neurons, and neurons were selected for grid Mod I, II, III, and IV, respectively.  

# Statistics  

To quantify the significance of hexagonal pattern regularity, grid score distribution was created using field-shuffling permutation (Fig.S9; Krupic et al., 2012). The significance threshold  

of grid scores was derived from the distribution. Specifically, the hexagonal patterns generated in the Reverse-replay condition, which produced on average the highest grid scores across the conditions, were used for field-shuffling permutation. The original spatial structures of each hexagonal pattern were shuffled by randomly changing the positions of receptive fields 1000 times with the local structure (the shape of receptive fields) preserved. The uncorrected threshold was determined by the 99$^{th}$ percentile of the grid scores of shuffled hexagonal patterns. The significant threshold corrected for multiple comparisons was further determined by the 95$^{th}$ percentile of the uncorrected-threshold distribution of hexagonal pattern population. The same procedure was used for persistent cohomology analysis, where the uncorrected threshold and the corrected threshold for multiple comparisons were determined by the 99th and 95th percentiles of the random distribution of barcodes, estimated based on the shuffled activity patterns of the receptive field, respectively. The significance thresholds were finally averaged across grid scales. The grid score differences between small scale (Mod I & Mod II) and large scale (Mod III & Mod IV) hexagonal patterns were examined using two sample t-test, two-tailed. The impact of the type of experience replay, amount of experience, and grid scales on grid scores were examined using ANOVA. Pearson correlation was used to examine the linear relationship of grid scores' decay slope with grid scale and amount of experience, respectively.
