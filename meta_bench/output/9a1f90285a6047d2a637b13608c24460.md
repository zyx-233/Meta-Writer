# Abstract  

Novel proteins can originate de novo from non-coding DNA and contribute to species-specific adaptations$^{1}$. Yet it remains unclear how these young proteins, whose sequences are entirely new to nature, integrate into pre-existing cellular systems to bring about beneficial traits. Here, we show that beneficial de novo emerging proteins (BEPs) in yeast$^{2}$ exploit conserved membrane-targeting, trafficking and degradation pathways to integrate and be maintained within cells. Using genetic, biochemical, and cell biological approaches, we find that BEPs preferentially localize to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Despite having unrelated sequences, ER-localized BEPs converge on a shared protein feature—a C-terminal transmembrane domain—that enables recognition by conserved post-translational ER insertion pathways. From the ER, BEPs traffic from the ER to ultimately accumulate in the vacuole. Their homeostasis is regulated by conserved proteasomal and vacuolar degradation pathways. Our findings demonstrate that ancient membrane-associated systems can process young de novo proteins bearing a convergent protein feature. These pathways may act as selective gateways, shaping which young de novo proteins persist. Membranes may therefore offer a "safe harbor"$^{3}$ for the cellular integration of certain classes of de novo proteins, thereby serving as a cradle for evolutionary innovation.  

# Main  

New protein-coding genes can evolve de novo from sequences that were previously non-genic. Once considered rare, de novo gene birth is now recognized as a major force of molecular innovation and adaptation across species$^{1,4-6}$. However, the mechanisms by which these de novo proteins integrate into cellular systems to provide fitness benefits remain poorly understood$^{6,7}$. Ancient proteins have been coevolving for millions of years with the systems that help them fold and localize correctly, regulate their homeostasis, and enable their beneficial activities$^{8-11}$. How are de novo proteins — naïve to these systems — recognized and processed by the cell such that their expression is not only tolerated, but beneficial (Fig 1A)?  

We and others have hypothesized that membranes may offer a privileged entry point for de novo proteins to gain access to the cellular environment$^{2,3,12-14}$. Hydrophobic protein motifs arise frequently from non-coding DNA$^{2,15,16}$, which might facilitate de novo protein localization to membrane-bound organelles$^{2,3,12-14}$. Membranes could allow hydrophobic de novo proteins to evolve local beneficial interactions while limiting potentially deleterious aggregation within the cytoplasm$^{2,3,14}$. Several lines of evidence support this idea. In yeast, beneficial de novo emerging proteins (BEPs) that promote growth under nutrient stress are enriched in predicted transmembrane domains (TMDs)$^{2}$. Although the subcellular localization of most de novo proteins is unknown, we found that one BEP inserts into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane$^{2}$. A disproportionate fraction of de novo open-reading frames is transcriptionally co-expressed with membrane-associated genes$^{17}$. In humans, newly identified microproteins—many of which have evolved de novo$^{18}$—exhibit a high prevalence of predicted TMDs$^{14}$. In arctic fish, a de novo protein exerts life-saving anti-freeze activity thanks to its ability to traffic through secretory pathway membranes$^{19}$. Despite these compelling observations across diverse organisms, no study to date has mapped how de novo proteins interact with the cell in the early stages of their transition between non-coding and coding DNA. Further, the role of membranes in de novo protein emergence and cellular integration remains experimentally undefined.  

Here, we demonstrate for the first time that beneficial de novo proteins can access membranes via recognition and processing by ancient cellular machinery. We leveraged yeast BEPs as a model set of de novo proteins and defined their specific subcellular localizations, targeting, and degradation mechanisms. We find that BEPs preferentially localize to membrane-bound compartments, with a strong enrichment at the ER. BEP targeting to the ER depends on the conserved post-translational targeting machinery (Guided Entry of Tail-anchored proteins (GET) and Srp-iNDependent (SND) pathways, while BEP homeostasis at the ER is regulated by the conserved ER-associated degradation pathway (ERAD). We further show that ER-localized BEPs can exit the ER through the secretory pathway to reach the vacuole (the yeast lysosome equivalent). Remarkably, despite being entirely distinct at the sequence level, all ER-localized BEPs share a predicted C-terminal TMD that appears to enable engagement with conserved targeting pathways. This convergence on similar structural features, targeting mechanisms, subcellular localization and modes of degradation point to a common evolutionary route for de novo proteins to integrate into the cell: through membranes and by harnessing ancient regulatory pathways. Over evolutionary timescales, these ancient pathways may act as a filter to constrain which de novo emerging sequences persist and contribute molecular innovation.  

# BEPs preferentially localize to the ER and are susceptible to degradation  

We first addressed how BEPs integrate into the cell by asking where they localize. To this aim, we made 26 C-terminal BEP-EGFP fusions and expressed them on plasmids under the control of an inducible promoter$^{20-22}$. Microscopy revealed that a majority of BEPs (18/26) localized to membranous compartments (ER, mitochondria, nucleus, and plasma membrane) in at least some cells (Fig 1B). The ER was overrepresented among these compartments (Fig 1B and Fig S1). Seven out of twenty-six BEPs (27%) showed enriched ER localization in contrast to only \~8% of the ancient yeast proteome (Fisher exact test, odds ratio= 4.4, p= 3.1 x 10$^{-3}$; Fig 1C)$^{23}$. Together these results demonstrate that while BEPs localize to a wide variety of cellular compartments, they are enriched in the ER.  

In some cells, BEPs displayed diffuse, punctate, or no measurable fluorescence (Fig 1B). This could be due to plasmid loss, reduced transcript stability, reduced translation efficiency, or poor protein stability. Immunoblotting revealed a broad distribution of protein abundances and breakdown products (Fig S2), suggesting that BEPs are highly susceptible to degradation.  

Given the unexpectedly high number of BEPs observed at the ER, we compared the phenotypic impacts of overexpressing ER-localized BEPs and other BEPs. ER-localized BEPs provided growth benefits across a broader array of nutrient stress conditions$^{2}$ than other BEPs (Mann Whitney U test, p= 0.025, n= 26; Fig 1D). These results reveal a strong association between ER localization and growth benefits among de novo emerging proteins in yeast. In what follows, we investigated how BEPs can be successfully targeted to the ER, as well as how their protein homeostasis can be regulated, despite possessing novel sequences that have had minimal opportunity to co-evolve with the cellular machinery.  

# ER-localized BEPs possess C-terminal transmembrane domains  

First, we searched for potential molecular determinants within BEPs' amino acid sequences that may distinguish ER-localized BEPs from other BEPs. Most BEPs (21/26), whether they are ER localized or not, are predicted to encode TMDs by both Phobius$^{24}$ and TMHMM$^{25}$ (Fig S3)$^{2}$. Structural predictions by Alpha-Fold2$^{26}$ were consistent with these predictions revealing widespread alpha helices throughout BEP structures (Fig S4). Thus, all the ER-localized BEPs display the biophysical potential to integrate into membranes due to the presence of predicted TMDs, but this feature alone cannot explain their specific ER localization, as it is also present in BEPs that localize elsewhere.  

We therefore asked whether the protein sequences of ER-localized BEPs possessed unique biochemical properties distinguishing them from other BEPs. Proteins that access the ER co-translationally often contain a signal sequence to help direct them to the translocon$^{27}$. However, no signal sequences were predicted in the ER-localized BEPs by TargetP 2.0$^{28}$ or SignalP 6.0$^{29}$. Alternatively, ER proteins can be synthesized in the cytoplasm and then post-translationally inserted in the ER using the SND and/or GET pathways$^{30}$. These pathways recognize TMDs in the middle or the C-terminus of proteins, respectively$^{30}$. In addition, membrane proteins targeted to the ER tend to have more hydrophobic TMDs and less positively charged C-tail extensions (CTEs) than proteins targeted to alternative membranes like the mitochondria$^{31,32}$. To look for potential molecular determinants of ER localization in BEP sequences, we therefore measured the length, hydrophobicity, and positive charge in different sequence regions from all BEPs with predicted TMDs. We considered separately their full-length sequences, regions corresponding to their N or C terminal ends, or their predicted TMDs (Fig 2A).  

We found that all 3 features cleanly distinguished ER-localized BEPs from the other TMD-containing BEPs. Consistent with an post-translational ER-insertion mechanism, ER  

localized BEPs were predicted to have TMDs near their C-terminus, with short CTEs less than 26 amino acids$^{31,33}$ beyond the last predicted TMD (Fig S3). The predicted CTEs of ER-localized BEPs were significantly shorter than those of other TMD-containing BEPs (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.036, effect size r = Z/$\sqrt{N}$ = 0.48; Fig 2B, S5A). In contrast, the overall lengths of ER-localized BEPs, as well as the lengths of their N-termini or TMDs, were statistically indistinguishable from those of other TMD-containing BEPs (Fig 2B, S5A). In addition to being shorter, the CTEs after the final TMD of ER-localized BEPs were also significantly less positively charged and more hydrophobic than those of other TMD-containing BEPs (Fig 2C, Fig S5B-E). The relative paucity of positively charged residues agrees with previous work on distinguishing characteristics of post-translationally targeted ER tail-anchored (TA) proteins in yeast$^{32}$. The increased CTE hydrophobicity of ER-localized BEPs (Fig 2C, Fig S5D-F) is unexpected.  

Upon closer inspection of the predicted structures (Fig S4), it becomes apparent that while several BEPs have unstructured CTEs, all ER-localized BEPs' CTEs end with either just a few hydrophobic residues after the TMD or an alpha-helical motif. Therefore, given the uncertainty in TMD boundary predictions$^{34}$ (Fig S3), some of the residues in the predicted CTEs of ER-localized BEPs could in fact belong to the their C-terminal TMDs. If so, the true TMDs of ER-localized BEPs would be even closer to their C termini than suggested by sequence-based predictions – which are already highly compatible with post-translational insertion mechanisms$^{33}$ (Fig 2C, Fig S5F).  

Together our analyses show that all ER-localized BEPs are predicted to contain TMDs near their C-termini. Our results demonstrate that these evolutionarily novel proteins with unique primary sequences nevertheless share common protein features in their C-terminal regions. We thus hypothesized that these C-terminal regions serve as molecular determinants for BEP ER targeting and experimentally tested this hypothesis in a model ER-localized BEP.  

# The C-terminal region of a model BEP is sufficient for ER targeting  

To experimentally test our hypothesis, we focused on the ER-localized BEP Ybr196c-a. This protein is a good model BEP for ER targeting as we previously showed that it is a bona-fide integral ER membrane protein$^{2}$. Ybr196c-a has one of the longest predicted CTEs among all ER-localized BEPs (26 residues; Fig S3). We suspected that this predicted CTE might mask a second TMD that failed to be predicted since the C-terminus of Ybr196c-a was predicted to be alpha-helical (Fig S4). Evolutionary analyses of the Ybr196c-a locus across budding yeast species are also consistent with a second TMD$^{2}$, which is predicted in the homologous region of Ybr196c-a's orthologue in S. paradoxus$^{2}$.  

To map the sequence determinants that dictate Ybr196c-a's ER localization, we assessed the localization of the full length and two discrete fragments of each orthologous Ybr196c-a sequence from S. cerevisiae and S. paradoxus spanning from: 1) the N-terminus to the end of the S. cerevisiae's predicted TMD (Ybr196c-a 1.1), and 2) the end of the S. cerevisiae's predicted TMD to the C-terminus of either species' sequences (Ybr196c-a 1.2) (Fig 2D). We integrated the full-length and partial sequences (1.1 and 1.2) fused with mNG into the S. cerevisiae genome under the control of an inducible promoter$^{22}$. The full-length Ybr196c-a of both S. cerevisiae and S. paradoxus localized to the ER in 100% of cells (Fig 2E, left). In both species, the N-terminal sequence, Ybr196c-a 1.1, localized to the mitochondria, while the C-terminal sequence, Ybr196c-a 1.2, displayed clear localization to the ER (Fig 2E, right). These results suggest that the CTE of Ybr196c-a in S. cerevisiae contains a TMD that was missed by computational prediction yet enables integration into the ER membrane$^{2}$. The fact that the C-terminal region of our model ER-localized BEP dictates its targeting to the ER  

is consistent with known mechanisms of post-translational insertion for conserved proteins$^{30}$. Therefore, de novo emerging proteins possess features enabling ER localization that are akin to those of conserved proteins, suggesting they may use similar strategies for ER targeting.  

# ER targeting of BEPs depends on conserved post-translational machinery  

To determine if our model ER-localized BEP, Ybr196c-a, reaches the ER via a canonical co- or post-translational targeting mechanism, we evaluated whether its localization was altered upon perturbing the three major eukaryotic ER targeting pathways (SRP-dependent, GET, and SND). To this aim, we engineered relevant gene deletions into strains expressing a chromosomally integrated Ybr196c-a-mNG fusion under the control of an inducible promoter, as in Fig 2E. Upon disruption of the core GET or SND machinery (Get1-3 or Snd2-3, respectively), fluorescence signal at the ER was reduced and an elevated number of cytosolic puncta was observed (Fig 3A-C, Fig S6A). This punctate localization pattern is expected for protein clients of these post-translational insertion pathways, as disruption of these targeting mechanisms results in cytosolic aggregation of client proteins$^{35,36}$. In contrast, disruption of GET pathway accessory proteins (e.g., Get4, Get5 and Sgt2) or the co-translational SRP-dependent targeting components (e.g., Sbh1, Sec71, and Sec72) did not alter Ybr196c-a localization (Fig S6A). Ybr196c-a's reliance on operational GET and SND systems for its ER targeting was more apparent with the N-terminal than the C-terminal mNG-tagged forms (Fig 3A-C, Fig S6A). Perturbing GET or SND components had no impact on free mNG localization, ruling out effects of the fluorescent tag on Ybr196c-a's targeting (Fig S6B). Together, these findings demonstrate that Ybr196c-a depends on functional GET or SND machinery to access the ER.  

To determine if these findings generalize to the other ER-localized BEPs, we examined their localization in the absence of Get3 and Snd3 using chromosomally integrated C and N-terminal mNG-fusions under the control of an inducible promoter. We found that, like Ybr196c-a, all the ER-localized BEPs were dependent on the GET or SND core machinery for their targeting to the ER (Fig 3B-C and Fig S6C-D). This effect was more pronounced when the BEPs were N-terminally tagged and their C-termini were accessible (Fig 3B-C). We hypothesize that this asymmetry is caused by the C-terminal mNG tags masking the TMDs needed for efficient post-translational ER insertion. Indeed, a similar effect as has been reported for other proteins$^{37}$ and linked to a favored topology of insertion where the C terminal end more efficiently translocates through the membrane$^{33}$. Together these results reveal that all ER-localized BEPs depend on the core components of the GET and SND post-translational targeting pathways to reach the ER. While the BEPs are evolutionarily novel, the GET and SND pathways are conserved across eukarya$^{38}$.  

# Conserved ERAD pathways regulate BEP stability at the ER  

Having established that BEPs depend on conserved pathways for ER localization, we next assessed whether they also rely on conserved pathways for their homeostasis. To this aim we first asked whether the abundance of our model ER-localized BEP, Ybr196c-a, depends on the two major ER-resident E3 ubiquitin (Ub) ligases involved in ERAD that specifically manage the surveillance of membrane proteins, Doa10 and Hrd1$^{39}$. Immunoblotting for Ybr196c-a tagged either N- or C-terminally with mNG revealed a large increase in Ybr196c-a levels upon $DOA10$ deletion (Fig 4A-B). This increase was not driven by the tag: free mNG was not stabilized by the loss of $DOA10$ (Fig S7A-B), and a similar increase upon deletion of $DOA10$  

was observed when Ybr196c-a was fused to an HA tag (Fig S7C-D). We then examined the protein turnover of Ybr196c-a by blocking translation with the addition of cycloheximide (CHX)$^{40}$ and monitoring its protein abundance over time. We performed these CHX-chase assays using: 1) C-terminal mNG-tagged Ybr196c-a (Fig 4C-D), 2) free mNG (Fig S7E-F), 3) C-terminal HA-tagged Ybr196c-a (Fig S7G-H), and 4) N-terminal mNG-tagged Ybr196c-a (Fig S7I-J). From these assays, we confirmed that all versions of Ybr196c-a (N- or C-terminally-tagged with either mNG or HA) were stabilized by loss of Doa10. Doa10 ubiquitination of ER-resident proteins often results in their retrotranslocation from the ER and subsequent proteasomal degradation in the cytosoplasm$^{41}$. Accordingly, we found that Ybr196c-a was more stable in the presence of a proteasome inhibitor than in vehicle control-treated cells (Fig 4E-F), unlike free mNG (Fig S7K) and irrespective of the positioning of the mNG tag (Fig S7L). Together, these experiments demonstrate that the protein stability of our model ER-localized BEP depends on the ER-resident Ub ligase Doa10 and proteasome-dependent degradation.  

To confirm our findings and determine if they generalize to the other ER-localized BEPs, we examined their abundance using microscopy in the absence of Doa10 and/or Hrd1 using chromosomally integrated C- and N-terminal mNG fusions under the control of an inducible promoter. Using this approach, we confirmed that Ybr196c-a abundance and ER localization increases in the absence of Doa10, irrespective of tag location (Fig 5A-B). Quantification of whole-cell fluorescence from these images revealed Ybr196c-a's abundance also significantly increased upon loss of Hrd1, but less so than in the Doa10 deletion cells (Fig 5C). Like Ybr196c-a, we found that all the ER-localized BEPs increased in abundance and ER localization upon loss of at least one of the Ub ligases; immunoblotting revealed distinct bands (Fig S8A-E), and microscopy revealed increased whole cell fluorescence and ER localization (Fig S9). Based on immunoblotting and microscopy, protein stabilization was more pronounced for N-terminally tagged BEPs, which would make their C-termini accessible (Fig 5D, Fig S8-S9). While a larger fraction of cells with distinguishable ER localization was observed for all C-terminally tagged BEPs upon Ub ligase deletion, whole-cell fluorescence increased for only three of these BEPs (Fig 5D, Fig S9). Collectively these results demonstrate that the homeostasis of all ER-localized BEPs depends on the major ER-resident Ub ligases, which are conserved across all three kingdoms of life$^{42}$.  

# Vacuolar protein sorting machinery mediates downstream BEP homeostasis  

Finally, we explored the possibility that the homeostasis of ER-localized BEPs might also be regulated through proteasome-independent mechanisms in the vacuole, the yeast equivalent of the lysosome$^{43}$. In the absence of the master vacuolar protease Pep4, fluorescently-tagged proteins targeted to this organelle accumulate vacuolar fluorescence because the fluorophore is not degraded efficiently$^{44-47}$. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that all ER-localized BEPs accumulate in the vacuole when Pep4 is deleted, suggesting they are all able to transit the secretory pathway to the vacuole (Fig 6A-B and Fig S10A-G). The two known transit routes from the ER to the vacuole are via ER-phagy or vesicle-mediated trafficking$^{48}$. In the case of vesicular trafficking, disruption of vacuolar protein sorting via loss of Vps4, an ESCRT-III (endosomal-sorting complex required for transport) component$^{49}$, will prevent sorting of membrane proteins into multi-vesicular bodies and block their delivery into the vacuole lumen. If ER-localized BEPs rely on vesicle-mediated trafficking to the vacuole, then loss of Vps4 will block their accumulation in the vacuolar lumen, instead leading to their accumulation in endosomes adjacent to the vacuole$^{50}$. Consistently, deletion of Vps4 in cells lacking Pep4 prevented accumulation of mNG signal in the vacuole and led to the formation of large fluorescent puncta adjacent to the vacuole (Fig 6C-D, Fig S10H-L). These findings imply that  

ER-localized BEPs traffic through the secretory and/or endocytic pathways beyond the ER. Altogether our results demonstrate that the homeostasis of ER-localized BEPs is not only dependent on the ERAD pathway and the proteasome, but also on the vacuolar protein sorting machinery (Fig 6E), each of which predate the origin of eukaryotes$^{51}$.  

# Discussion  

In this study, we report the first experimental investigation of cellular processing of de novo proteins, revealing the early stages of their cellular integration. Our results demonstrate that ancient pathways control the targeting and homeostasis of a group of yeast BEPs. BEPs exploit ancient pathways through simple biochemical features encoded in their primary sequences—features common in non-coding DNA$^{2,15,16}$ and accessible without extensive prior adaptation.  

We propose that the shared cellular processing observed here for ER-localized BEPs results from a common C-terminal TMD that enables recognition by ancient pathways (Fig 6E). Altogether, our findings suggest that de novo proteins display molecular convergence, whether neutrally or under the action of natural selection$^{52}$. Although distinct at the sequence level, ER-localized BEPs converge on similar structural features, targeting mechanisms, modes of degradation, and phenotypic outcomes, pointing to a common, favored route of integration in the cell. Over evolutionary timescales, such favored routes could act as filters for de novo emerging sequences, ultimately constraining molecular innovation. By channeling proteins through specific pathways, cells may bias the structural features—and thus the functional capacities—of proteins that successfully emerge de novo. As a result, the functional repertoire of young genes may represent a biased subset of the molecular activities conserved proteins can perform.  

The BEPs that we investigated only exist in the yeast lineage, yet we find the cellular machinery necessary for their targeting and homeostasis are deeply conserved. For example, the GET and SND post-translational membrane insertion pathways are conserved across eukarya$^{38}$. Further, the ERAD pathways is conserved in eukaryotes, archaea, and actinobacteria$^{42}$. Finally, all ER-localized BEPs also access the vacuole via an ESCRT-mediated mechanism that predates the last universal eukaryotic ancestor$^{51}$ (Fig 6E). Given the wide-spread occurrence of de novo gene birth$^{1}$, coupled with the fact that BEPs co-opt cellular pathways with deep conservation suggests that similar mechanisms of cellular integration may be exploited throughout diverse taxa.  

We uncovered a strong association between ER localization and growth benefit, offering the potential to investigate the mechanisms of BEP adaptability in future studies. However, our study focused on a set of BEPs discovered under nutrient stress conditions². The molecular signatures and cellular behaviors we observed may reflect traits that are uniquely beneficial in those environments. There are many thousands more putative de novo ORFs in the genome with evidence of translation⁵³. Some of these ORFs may encode proteins that confer benefits under conditions not yet tested and may achieve phenotypic effects through distinct cellular localizations mediated by other common protein features beyond C-terminal TMDs. Nonetheless, that BEPs harbor shared biochemical features suggests the future possibility of predicting their likelihood of cellular integration and genomic establishment.  

Recent studies have shown that hydrophobic C-termini in sequences undergoing noncanonical translation can act as signals for proteasomal degradation in humans, a process dependent on the Bag6 membrane protein triage complex$^{12,54}$. Bag6 serves as a decision point between cytosolic degradation and ER membrane targeting of tail-anchored proteins via its interaction with the Transmembrane Recognition Complex (TRC) pathway—the human equivalent of the yeast GET pathway$^{55}$. This dual role suggests Bag6 may both mitigate  

harmful effects of noncanonical translation and enable the evolution of novel membrane proteins$^{12,13}$. Although yeast lack a Bag6 ortholog, ER-localized BEPs with hydrophobic C-termini rely on the GET pathway for ER localization. It is possible that these same hydrophobic C-terminal regions involved in targeting somehow contribute to degradation, since we observed that BEP stabilization was strongly dependent on tag orientation. Regulated degradation makes it possible to modulate protein abundance and potentially mitigate negative side effects of higher expression levels. Thus, in an evolutionary context, the persistence of de novo proteins will depend not simply on whether they are degraded, but on the balance between degradation rates and expression levels: the ability to co-opt degradation pathways may represent a selective advantage for young de novo proteins.  

It remains unclear to what extent BEPs are born with the potential for cellular integration. In the case of Ybr196c-a, our previous experiments suggested that it emerged from a non-coding locus that already possessed the capacity to form a C-terminal TMD and that the efficiency of ER targeting may have increased since the ORF's initial de novo emergence². Similarly, in arctic fish, a secreted de novo protein enters the secretory pathway using a signal sequence that was present in the genome prior to the protein's birth from non-coding DNA¹⁹. Finally, non-genic sequences enriched in thymine tend to encode hydrophobic amino acids and have a high propensity to form TMDs²,¹⁵,¹⁶. Together these examples suggest it is plausible that de novo proteins regularly emerge with biochemical properties that support their membrane targeting. Future studies will be needed to determine the precise evolutionary mechanism by which BEPs acquired their capability to interface with the cell's machinery.  

Our findings offer new directions of research at the intersection of cell biology and de novo gene birth. For example, we show that the homeostasis of ER-localized BEPs depends both on the proteasome and the vacuole (Fig 6E). This implies that some BEPs escape the ER and enter the secretory pathway. Accordingly, ER-localized BEPs lack the canonical KDEL retrieval sequence$^{56}$ and so will not be retrieved if they are non-selectively incorporated into vesicles leaving the ER. Their passive incorporation into ER exit vesicles may be more likely under conditions of cargo crowding, which improves sorting fidelity and increases bulk flow$^{57}$. Similarly, it will be interesting to define the molecular pathway that takes BEPs to the vacuole. Our findings suggest that the pathway is ESCRT-dependent and therefore could be via Golgi- or endosome-to vacuole trafficking$^{58}$ or post-endocytic sorting from the plasma membrane to the vacuole$^{59}$. The trafficking route followed for BEPs could ultimately facilitate the evolution of novel activities for these proteins outside of the ER.  

Cellular context may also influence BEPs' molecular functions. When we dissected the coding sequence of Ybr196c-a, we found that the C-terminal sequence dictates its ER localization whereas the N-terminal sequence, and its single predicted TMD, localizes to the mitochondria. This suggests that two distinct targeting potentials exist within a single, small de novo protein. Consistent with this observation, ER and mitochondrial targeting pathways can actively compete for clients and the biochemical properties of amino acids surrounding the TMD profoundly influence membrane sorting $^{32,60,61}$. Understanding the precise molecular mechanisms of Ybr196c-a, and other BEP, targeting may prove useful in defining the biochemical parameters that dictate cargo recognition and targeting to distinct organelles. Mechanistic research on recently-emerged de novo proteins —unburdened by evolutionary history and homology—may improve our understanding and predictive ability in diverse areas of cellular biology.  

Together with others, we previously speculated that cellular membranes could serve as an entry point for cellular integration of young de novo proteins$^{2,3,12-14}$. In support of this hypothesis, we find a majority of BEPs are capable of localizing to membrane-bound organelles. Targeting to these organelles relies on the recognition of rudimentary biophysical characteristics within the primary amino acid sequence, making it somewhat promiscuous$^{61,62}$.  

Given such flexibility, select targeting and degradation pathways can even recognize random sequences$^{63-65}$. Permissive targeting has led some to propose that select organelles could provide an evolutionary "safe harbor" for incipient de novo proteins$^{3}$; We posit that membranes, particularly the ER membranes, serve as such a "safe harbor" for de novo emerging proteins, facilitating their evolutionary persistence and offering a prospective adaptive niche —thereby increasing the potential to acquire selected functions and become incorporated into the established genome.  

# Materials and Methods  

# Plasmids and DNA manipulations  

# Gateway entry clones  

To create a collection of entry clones for cloning with the Gateway System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), primers were designed containing the attB1 and attB2 sequences to amplify each ORF with a stop codon for the N' terminal tagging and without, for the C' terminal tagging. The primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA) and used to amplify each ORF using Q5 High-Fidelity 2x Master Mix (M0492L, New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA). Genomic DNA from the strain FY4 was extracted with a Yeast DNA Extraction kit (78870, Thermo Fisher) and used as a template for the PCR. The PCR conditions were as follows: 98°C for 30 s, followed by 25 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 15 s and 72°C for 30 s and a final elongation step of 2 min at 72°C. The PCR products were purified with NucleoFast 96 PCR Plates (743100.10, Macherey Nagel, Allentown, PA) and quantified on a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher) prior to use for recombination with the donor plasmid pDONR223, using BP Clonase II Enzyme mix (11789100, ThermoFisher). The recombination reactions were used to transform DH5α competent cells (C2987U, New England BioLabs) and positive clones were selected in LB media$^{66}$ supplemented with 50 µg/ml of spectinomycin (158993, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA). The NucleoSpin 96 Plasmid kit (740625.4, Macherey Nagel) was used to extract the plasmids and DNA quantification was done using the SpectraMax M4 plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).  

# Destination plasmids  

Two sets of destination plasmids were used in this study. The first set from the Yeast Gateway kit (1000000011, Addgene, Watertown, MA$^{67}$) includes the plasmids pAG415-GAL-ccdB-EGFP and pAG415-GAL-EGFP-ccdB. The second set was built to swap the GAL promoter with the Z$_3$EV promoter and the ccdB-EGFP was replaced with ccdB-mNG-Tadh1 and a hygromycin cassette (for the C terminally tag) or mNG-ccdB-Tadh1and a hygromycin cassette (for the N terminally tag), creating the destination plasmids pARC0031 and pARC0152.  

# Expression plasmids  

Expression plasmids were made by LR recombination between the entry clones and the destination plasmids described above, using the LR Clonase II enzyme mix (11791020,  

Thermo Fisher). The recombination reactions were used to transform DH5α competent cells (C2987U, New England BioLabs) and positive clones were selected in LB media supplemented with 100 µg/ml of ampicillin (J60977, Alfa Aesar, Haverfill, MA). The NucleoSpin 96 Plasmid kit (740625.4, Macherey Nagel) was used to extract the plasmids and DNA quantification was done using the SpectraMax M4 plate reader (Molecular Devices). We attempted to create expression plasmids for all 28 BEPs from Vakirlis et al², but only succeeded in constructing a final plasmid containing a BEP-EGFP fusion for 26 of the BEPs (YGR296W and YOR011WA failed). Plasmids used in this work are described in Supplemental Table 1.  

# Yeast strains and growth conditions  

The yeast strains used are described in Supplemental Table 2 and are all derived from S288c genetic backgrounds of S. cerevisiae.  

Yeast cells were grown in either synthetic complete medium (SC) lacking the appropriate amino acids for plasmid selection, prepared as described in$^{68}$ and using ammonium sulfate as a nitrogen source, or YPD$^{66}$ medium where indicated. Liquid medium was filter-sterilized and solid plate medium had 2% agar (w/v) added before autoclaving. When necessary for selection, hygromycin B or G-418 (H75020-1.0 and G64000-5.0, Research Products International, Mt Prospect, IL) was added to the media to a final concentration of 200 µg/ml. Yeast cells were grown at 30°C and where appropriate, 10-20 µM of β-estradiol (E2758-1G, Millipore-Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was added to cultures for 3 hours to induce expression from the $GEV$ or $Z_{3}EV$ promoters$^{22}$.  

# Yeast Transformation  

Strains containing plasmids overexpressing fusions with EGFP were transformed in the BY4741 background with or without $ACT1pr$-Z$_3$EV-NatMX to be used with β-estradiol or galactose induction, respectively. All other strains were built based on the DBY12394 containing the $ACT1pr$-Z$_3$EV-NatMX expression system generously provided by the Noyes lab$^{22}$.  

The constructs or DNA cassettes amplified from plasmids were integrated in the genome using the lithium acetate (LiAc), polyethyleneglycol (PEG) and salmon sperm DNA transformation protocol$^{69}$ with an adaptation for high-throughput scale. The yeast strain was grown in 1ml of YPD media in 96-deep-well plates and used for transformation with the liquid-handler EVO 150 (Tecan Group Ltd, Switzerland). Cells were washed in 750 μl of water, followed by 1 ml of LiAc tris-EDTA (TE) buffer and finally resuspended in 500 μl of the same buffer. Transformations were carried out using 5 μl of ssDNA (2 mg/ml) with 1 μg of purified PCR product or 500 ng of plasmid and 50 μl of cells. 250 μl of PLATE (40% PEG, 0.1M LiAc, 10mM Tris-HCl, 1mM EDTA) was added to each cell mixture and incubated at 30°C for 15 min, followed by 60 min at 42°C. Cells were then pelleted, resuspended in 200 μl of YPD or SC media for a recovery step at 30°C for 3 h, prior to being plated as 3 μl drops on selective medium. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 2-4 days until transformants grew. Agar plates were then used to pin into liquid media with the Singer RoToR (Singer Instruments, Somerset, UK) and then used to make glycerol stocks.  

The constructs containing the $Z_{3}EVpr$ followed by the coding sequences for individual BEPs tagged with mNG at the N' or C' terminus were amplified using the expression plasmids as template and integrated at the $HIS3$ chromosomal locus. Amplification was carried out using Q5 High-Fidelity 2x Master Mix (M0492L, New England BioLabs) and primers ARC338  

(TCTATATTTTTTTTATGCCTCGGTAATGATTTTCATTTTTTTTTTTTTCCACCTAGCGGA TGACTCTTTTTTTTTTCTTAGCGATTGGCATTATCACATAAATGAATTATACATTATA TAAAGTAATGTGATTTCCTTCGAAGAATATACTAAAAAATGAGCAGGCAAGATAA ACGAAGGCAAAGacaaaagctggagctctagta) and ARC339 (AAAGAAAAAGCAAAAAGAAAAAGGAAAGGCGCCTCGTTTCAGAATGACACGT ATAGAATGATGCATTACCTTGTCATCTTCAGTATCATACTGTTTCGTATACATACTT ACTGACATTCCATAGGTATACATATACACATGTATATATATCGTATGCTGCAGC TTAAATAATCGGTGTCAgcgaattgggtaccggcc) in a 50 μl reaction. The PCR conditions were as follows: 98°C for 30 s, followed by 25 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 56°C for 15s and 72°C for 2 min and a final elongation step of 5 min at 72°C. The PCR products were incubated at 37°C with 1μl of DpnI (R0176S, New England BioLabs) and then purified with NucleoFast 96 PCR Plates (743100.10, Macherey Nagel) and the DNA was quantified on a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher) prior to being used for yeast transformation. We were not successful in integrating YLL006W with either an N- or C-terminal mNG, nor for YMR151W with a C-terminal mNG, leaving 6 BEPs with mNG tagged on the N terminus and 5 with a C-terminal mNG tag.  

Gene deletions were performed with the same transformation protocol and using DNA cassettes ($URA3$ or $KANMX$) targeted to the region of interest using primers containing sequences homologous to the genomic locus.  

# Yeast Protein Extraction and Immunoblot Analyses  

Whole cell extracts of yeast proteins were generated using trichloroacetic acid (TCA) method as described in$^{70}$ and modified from$^{71}$. In brief, cells were grown in SC medium to mid-exponential log phase at 30°C ($A_{600} = 0.6$-$1.0$) and an equal density of cells was harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until processing. Cells were lysed using sodium hydroxide, precipitated with 50% TCA, solubilized in SDS/Urea buffer [8 M Urea, 200 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 0.1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 100 mM DTT, 100 mM Tris (not pH adjusted)] and heated to 37°C for 15 min prior to loading on an SDS-PAGE gel. Immunoblotted proteins were detected using mouse anti-green fluorescent protein (GFP) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), mNeonGreen (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) or HA antibodies (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). Anti-mouse secondary antibodies conjugated to IRDye-800 or IRDye-680 were used to detect primary GFP or mNG antibodies on an Odyssey CLx infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). The HRP-conjugated anti-HA antibody was detected on a ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). As a loading and transfer control, membranes were stained with Revert (LI-COR Biosciences) and detected using the Odyssey CLx.  

Extracts containing HA-tagged BEPs were loaded on 16.5% Tris-tricine gels and run in the cold using 1X tricine running buffer (Bio-Rad). Proteins were blotted to Immobilon-PSQ (0.2 micron) PVDF membrane using the Criterion system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were stained with REVERT total protein stain (LI-COR Biosciences), followed by blocking with TBST with 3% BSA and overnight incubation at 4°C on a platform rocker with an HRP-conjugated anti-HA antibody in TBST containing 1 % BSA (1:5000 dilution, Roche). Membranes were then washed 3 times with TBST and detected using the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher) on the Bio-Rad Chemidoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad).  

# Protein Stability Assays  

The stability of mNG- or HA-tagged YBR196c-a (as an N-terminal or C-terminal fusion) or mNG alone expressed as a chromosomal integration from the $Z_{3}EVpr$ was assessed by cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay as described in$^{40}$. Cells were grown to mid-exponential phase and induced to express the tagged YBR196c-a or mNG using β-estradiol. Cells were next treated with 150 µg/ml CHX (Gold Bio, St. Louis, MO) and equal densities of cells were harvested at the indicated times. Proteins were extracted from cell pellets and subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting, as described above.  

For assays that employed the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (Thermo Fisher), cells were incubated with 10 µM of MG-132 (stock 10 mM in DMSO) or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 1 h prior to CHX addition. When CHX was added to block new protein synthesis the t=0 timepoint was harvested to initiate the time course.  

# Fluorescence microscopy  

For imaging experiments, cells were grown and induced to express mNG-tagged BEPs or free mNG as indicated above. Fluorescent proteins were localized using: 1) epifluorescence microscopy, 2) confocal microscopy in low-throughput, or 3) confocal microscopy in high-throughput. For epifluorescence microscopy and low-throughput confocal microscopy, cells were stained with 70 μM Cell Tracker Blue CMAC dye (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and 10 μM trypan blue (Gibco, Dublin, Ireland) and plated onto 35 mm glass bottom microwell dishes that were concanavalin A (MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH) or poly-D-lysine coated (MatTek Corporation, Ashland, MA). For epifluorescence microscopy, cells were imaged using a Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope (Nikon, Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan) outfitted with an Orca Flask 4.0 cMOS camera (Hammamatsu, Bridgewater, NJ) and a 100x objective (NA 1.49). For low-throughput confocal microscopy, images were captured using a Nikon Ti2 A1R inverted microscope (Nikon) equipped with a 100x objective (NA 1.49) and detected with GaAsP or multi-alkali photomultiplier tube detectors. For high-throughput confocal microscopy, imaging was done as described in Bowman et al.$^{72}$.  

In all cases, image acquisition was controlled using NIS-Elements software (Nikon) and all images within an experiment were captured using identical settings. Images were cropped and adjusted equivalently using NIS-Elements (Nikon), and where added adjusted images are needed to capture the range of fluorescence intensities in an experiment, additional images are presented that are also evenly adjusted and the change in this adjustment is indicated in the figure panel.  

# Fluorescence microscopy image analysis and statistical tests  

To define the localizations represented for both EGFP- and mNG-tagged BEPs, all images were analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) with the Cell Counter plugin to categorize the localization for every cell in a field. These manually defined localization patterns are summarized in the pie charts presented in Figures 1B, 2E, 5A-B, S9A-B,D-E,G,I-J, and L-M.  

To quantify the whole cell fluorescence intensity for mNG-tagged BEPs, we used Nikon NIS-Elements .ai (Artificial Intelligence) and Nikon General Analysis 3 (GA3) software packages. We trained imaging on a ground-truth set of samples, where cells were segmented using images acquired with the trypan blue-stained cell surfaces or the DIC images. Next, the  

NIS.ai software performed iterative training until a training loss threshold of <0.2 was obtained, which is indicative of a high degree of agreement between the initial ground truth and the output generated by NIS.ai. Fields of images were then processed, using the automated segmentation, and the mean fluorescence in the green channel (480 nm) was measured for each cell. Any partial cells at the edges of images were removed. Fluorescence intensities are plotted as scatter plots using Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). We performed one-way ANOVA statistical tests with Dunnette's post hoc correction for multiple comparisons, and or Mann-Whitney tests when only two samples were compared. In all cases, significant p-values from these tests are represented as: * <0.05; ** <0.0005, *** <0.0005; ns >0.05.  

# Biochemical properties, transmembrane domain prediction, and statistics  

Amino acid sequences of the BEPs were analyzed for their biochemical properties using Python. A custom script that used the packages Biopython and 'peptides' was used to calculate hydrophobicity, percent charged residues, and length in different sequence regions. Transmembrane domain prediction was conducted by accessing the Phobius (https://phobius.sbc.su.se) and TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) online servers$^{25}$. A FASTA file containing all the amino acid sequences was uploaded and both analyses were run using default parameters. All statistical tests were performed in Python using scipy.stats.

For proteins that exhibited more than one localization, if one was ER, than that protein was counted as "ER", otherwise it was placed in the "other".  

Fitness measurements for BEPs (shown in Fig 1D) were obtained and reanalyzed from$^{2}$. For each BEP we counted the number of conditions that it was beneficial relative to a control, with the minimum and maximum number being 1 and 5, respectively.
